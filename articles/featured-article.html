Tongues of Fire, Words of Order

Understanding the Gift of Tongues from Pentecost to Corinth

By Lesli D. Mize

Published August 5, 2026

Explore what Scripture teaches about the gift of tongues from Pentecost to Corinth, and why spiritual gifts must operate with understanding, discernment, order, and love.

The Need for Balance

Throughout my walk with Jesus, I have attended churches representing various denominations, traditions, and theological perspectives. Some welcomed the powerful movement of the Holy Spirit but lacked sound biblical teaching. Others faithfully taught Scripture yet seemed hesitant to acknowledge or welcome the Spirit’s active work among His people. Too often, I have witnessed an imbalance between biblical truth and openness to the Holy Spirit.

Scripture and the Holy Spirit were never intended to stand in opposition. The Spirit inspired the written Word, illuminates its truth, and empowers believers to live according to it. A genuine movement of the Spirit will never contradict Scripture, nor should faithful biblical teaching diminish His presence and work.

Proverbs 11:1 declares, “A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight.” Although this proverb speaks directly of dishonest measurements, it also reflects God’s character—He values truth, integrity, and proper order. Yet the Church can become unbalanced in both what it teaches and how those teachings are practiced.

On one side are cessationists, who generally believe certain miraculous gifts, including tongues, prophecy, and miraculous healing, were given for the foundational period of the early Church and ceased after the death of the apostle John, the last of the apostles. On the other hand are continuationists, charismatics, and Pentecostals, who believe these gifts remain active today. Within some expressions of these movements, however, spiritual experiences may be elevated beyond their biblical purpose or accepted without careful examination according to Scripture.

The deeper question, then, is not simply whether spiritual gifts continue today, but whether everything taking place within the Church honors Jesus Christ, agrees with Scripture, strengthens the body of believers, and reflects the character of God.

First Corinthians 14 speaks directly to this issue. Paul neither dismisses spiritual gifts nor permits their uncontrolled use. Instead, he teaches believers that the gifts of the Holy Spirit are to operate with understanding, discernment, order, and love. His concern is not merely whether spiritual gifts are present, but whether their use glorifies God and builds up His people.

The Promise of the Holy Spirit

In Joel 2:28, God promised, “It will come about after this that I will pour out My Spirit on all mankind.” John the Baptist later pointed toward the fulfillment of this promise. Although John baptized with water for repentance, he declared that the One coming after him—Jesus Christ—would “baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire” (Matthew 3:11).

Jesus Himself confirmed that the promised outpouring was approaching. Before His ascension, He instructed His disciples to remain in Jerusalem until they were “clothed with power from on high,” describing the Holy Spirit as “the promise of My Father” (Luke 24:49). He reminded them that although John had baptized with water, they would “be baptized with the Holy Spirit not many days from now” (Acts 1:5). Jesus then explained the purpose of that power: “but you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you; and you shall be My witnesses” (Acts 1:8).

The Day of Pentecost

Before Pentecost, about 120 believers had gathered in Jerusalem, including the apostles who had witnessed their Master suffer, die, rise from the grave, and ascend into heaven (Acts 1:15). In obedience to Christ’s instructions, they remained there awaiting the promised Holy Spirit—a gift they could not yet fully understand (Acts 1:4–5).

When the Day of Pentecost arrived, they were all together in one place. Suddenly, “a noise like a violent rushing wind” came from heaven and filled the entire house where they were sitting (Acts 2:1–2). Luke does not describe an actual wind swirling through the room; he describes a sound from heaven that resembled the force of a mighty wind.

Living on the plains of Oklahoma, I know how powerful and frightening the sound of rushing wind can be. When mighty winds surround my home, I remain alert for tornado warnings and listen carefully for any sign of approaching danger. Imagine, then, hearing a sound like a violent wind—not merely outside the house, but filling the place where you were sitting.

Then something even more extraordinary occurred. What appeared to be tongues “as of fire” separated and rested upon each person. They were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began speaking in other tongues as the Spirit enabled them (Acts 2:3–4). God’s presence was not limited to one apostle, leader, or specially selected person. The manifestation rested upon each of them, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit.

What an astonishing and awe-inspiring experience this must have been! The believers who had gathered in obedience and expectation were now witnessing the fulfillment of the Father’s promise. The Holy Spirit had come, and the Church was being empowered to carry the gospel of Jesus Christ to the world.

Understanding “Other Tongues”

The verses that follow help us understand what Luke meant by “other tongues.” The believers were speaking languages they had not naturally learned or been trained to speak. Jerusalem was filled with devout Jewish men from nations throughout the known world who had gathered for the Feast of Pentecost. When they heard the sound, a crowd came together to discover what was happening.

The people were bewildered because each person heard the believers speaking in his own native language. They asked, “Are not all these who are speaking Galileans? And how is it that we each hear them in our own language to which we were born?” (Acts 2:7–8). Luke then lists the many regions represented among the crowd, emphasizing the diversity of languages being spoken.

These Galilean believers had not studied or mastered all these languages. Yet, through the power of the Holy Spirit, they were proclaiming “the mighty deeds of God” in words that people from many nations could understand (Acts 2:11). Some in the crowd were amazed and perplexed, asking what this extraordinary event meant, while others mocked the believers and accused them of being drunk (Acts 2:12–13).

Peter explained that what the people had witnessed was the fulfillment of Joel’s prophecy: “This is what was spoken of through the prophet Joel: ‘And it shall be in the last days,’ God says, ‘That I will pour out My Spirit on all mankind’” (Acts 2:16–18). Pentecost was not an isolated or unexpected spiritual event. It was the fulfillment of the Father’s promise—proclaimed through the prophet Joel, announced by John the Baptist, and confirmed by Jesus Christ.

The Baptism of the Holy Spirit

Today, many believers describe this promised experience as the baptism of the Holy Spirit. This terminology comes directly from Scripture, which describes Jesus as the One who baptizes with the Holy Spirit (Matthew 3:11; Acts 1:5).

The disagreement among believers is therefore not primarily about whether the Bible uses the term, but about how the baptism of the Holy Spirit should be understood. Some teach that every believer is baptized by the Holy Spirit at the moment of salvation and is thereby placed into the body of Christ, citing 1 Corinthians 12:13. Others understand the baptism of the Holy Spirit as an empowering experience that may occur after salvation, as demonstrated by the disciples’ experience on the Day of Pentecost.

Whatever position one takes, Scripture clearly presents the Holy Spirit as essential to the life and mission of the Church. The Spirit unites believers with Christ, dwells within them, distributes spiritual gifts, transforms their character, and empowers them to become effective witnesses of Jesus Christ.

Tongues and the Evidence of Salvation

Within certain Christian traditions, speaking in tongues is taught as the initial evidence that a believer has been baptized with the Holy Spirit. Some groups go even further and treat speaking in tongues as necessary evidence of salvation. In the book of Acts, tongues did accompany the reception of the Holy Spirit in specific instances—such as when the Gentile believers received the Spirit in Acts 10:45–47 and spoke in tongues, and when the disciples in Ephesus received the Holy Spirit in Acts 19:1–6 and likewise spoke in tongues. However, although these passages show tongues accompanying the reception of the Spirit in particular cases, Scripture does not establish speaking in tongues as a universal requirement for either receiving the Holy Spirit or being saved.

Paul makes it clear that the Holy Spirit distributes different gifts to different members of the body according to His will (1 Corinthians 12:7–11). He later asks, “All do not speak with tongues, do they?” (1 Corinthians 12:30). The expected answer is no. If every believer were required to speak in tongues as proof of salvation or the Holy Spirit’s presence, Paul could not describe tongues as one gift distributed among many.

Salvation is received by grace through faith in Jesus Christ, not through the manifestation of a particular spiritual gift (Ephesians 2:8–9; Romans 10:9–10). Every person who belongs to Christ has the Spirit of Christ (Romans 8:9), but not every believer receives the same spiritual gifts. Therefore, speaking in tongues may be a genuine manifestation of the Holy Spirit, but it should not be treated as the universal evidence of salvation or as the sole evidence that the Holy Spirit is working in a believer’s life.

The Church in Corinth

In his first letter to the church in Corinth, Paul addressed the challenges believers faced as they left their former ways and learned to follow Jesus Christ. Many members of the congregation had come from a pagan culture shaped by idolatry, sexual immorality, social divisions, and various forms of religious worship. They were now learning an entirely new way of worshiping God and living in fellowship with one another.

Their conversion to Christ did not mean that every former belief, behavior, and cultural influence immediately disappeared. These influences followed them into the Church, creating confusion and disorder in their relationships, conduct, and corporate worship. Therefore, Paul instructed them concerning divisions within the congregation, sexual immorality, marriage, food offered to idols, the Lord’s Supper, spiritual gifts, orderly worship, and the resurrection. In chapters 12 through 14, he specifically addressed their understanding and use of spiritual gifts, teaching them that every gift must operate through love and for the edification of the entire Church.

Tongues and Interpretations in the Church

For those who have received the gift of various kinds of tongues, it is an extraordinary and mysterious gift of the Holy Spirit. Paul explains that the person who speaks in a tongue “does not speak to men, but to God” and edifies himself (1 Corinthians 14:2, 4), describing the personal exercise of the gift between the believer and God.

At Pentecost, the Spirit enabled believers to speak languages they had never learned, while people from many nations recognized their native languages and understood them proclaiming “the mighty deeds of God” (Acts 2:6–11). No interpreter was necessary because the listeners already understood what was being spoken.

In Corinth, however, tongues spoken publicly could not edify the Church unless their meaning was interpreted. The interpretation of tongues is itself a spiritual gift given by the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:10), not merely the natural ability to recognize or translate a language.

Paul did not forbid tongues—he spoke in tongues himself and instructed believers, “do not forbid to speak in tongues” (1 Corinthians 14:18, 39). Yet he established boundaries for their public use. If no interpreter was present, the speaker was to “keep silent in the church; and let him speak to himself and to God” (1 Corinthians 14:28).

The gift itself was not the problem; disorder was. Whether at Pentecost or Corinth, Paul’s principle is clear: when tongues are expressed publicly, their meaning must be understood so the Church can be edified.
