When the Tempter Speaks

Whose Voice Will You Follow?

Matthew 4:1–11

By Lesli D. Mize • Published September 30, 2026

Follow Jesus into the wilderness and learn how He answered temptation with Scripture, trusted the Father, and remained faithful through every test.

Read-Along Podcast

Listen as Lesli D. Mize narrates this featured message.

After John baptized Jesus, a voice from heaven declared, “This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well-pleased” (Matthew 3:17). Before anyone else could define who Jesus was, the Father had spoken and confirmed His Son. The Father’s words recall Psalm 2:7, which speaks of God’s Son, and Isaiah 42:1, which describes the servant in whom God delights.

Jesus in the Wilderness

Then the scene immediately changes. The Holy Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness, where He will be tempted by the devil (Matthew 4:1). Mark describes the movement more forcefully: the Spirit immediately drove Him there (Mark 1:12). However we picture that journey, one point is clear: Jesus did not aimlessly wander into the wilderness by mistake. The Spirit led Him there.

The wilderness was a demanding place in Israel’s story. Israel spent forty difficult years there, being humbled and tested to see whether they would keep God’s commandments (Deuteronomy 8:2–3). The wilderness is often described as a desolate, dangerous wasteland and a place of solitude. Yet it was not always a place of distress. Luke 5:16 tells us that Jesus often withdrew to the wilderness to pray. Sometimes a quiet place offers welcome relief from the noise of a crowd. This time, however, Jesus would face an adversary.

Matthew tells us that Jesus fasted for forty days and forty nights. Scripture does not present a fast of that length as an ordinary custom. Yet, Moses spent forty days and nights with the Lord on the mountain without food or water (Exodus 34:28). Matthew says Jesus fasted, and Luke says He ate nothing during those days; neither Gospel says He went without water.

The number forty appears in other significant periods. Such as when the spies explored the promised land for forty days (Numbers 13:25). Israel spent forty years in the wilderness (Deuteronomy 8:2). Jonah warned Nineveh that judgment would come in forty days (Jonah 3:4). These accounts do not give the number forty a single secret meaning that we can apply to every verse. They do, however, help us notice the weight of Jesus’ forty days in the wilderness.

Beginning in the first verse of Matthew 4, Matthew introduces Jesus’ opponent as the devil, calls him the tempter when he approaches Jesus (4:3), and later records Jesus addressing him as Satan (4:10). These names fit his actions in this encounter: he comes as an adversary, seeking to exploit Jesus’ identity as God’s Son, tempt Him to act apart from the Father’s will, and draw Him away from His purpose. The temptations Jesus faced reflect struggles we recognize in our own lives, like meeting our needs on our own terms, demanding proof from God, and taking shortcuts to what we desire. Jesus faced these temptations, yet remained faithful to the Father. When does the tempter approach? After forty days of fasting, when Jesus is hungry. Matthew includes that detail clearly. The tempter’s opening words show that this encounter will reach deeper than an empty stomach.

The First Temptation: More Than Bread

After forty days and nights of fasting, Jesus was hungry (Matthew 4:2). Matthew does not soften that detail. Jesus had a real physical need, and the tempter approached Him at a moment of weakness.

“If You are the Son of God, command that these stones become bread,” he said (Matthew 4:3).

At first glance, this sounds like a temptation about lunch. Bread would certainly have been welcomed. Yet the tempter did not simply say, “You must be hungry.” He began with Jesus’ identity: “If You are the Son of God.” The Father had already declared Jesus to be His beloved Son at His baptism (Matthew 3:17). Nothing in the passage suggests Jesus doubted that. The challenge was whether He would act as the Son on the tempter’s terms, using His power to meet His hunger at the tempter’s prompting.

Jesus answered with Scripture: “Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that proceeds out of the mouth of God” (Matthew 4:4). He was drawing from Deuteronomy 8:3, where Moses recalled how God let Israel experience hunger and then fed them with manna. Israel ate that manna throughout its wilderness years until they reached the land God had promised (Exodus 16:35; Joshua 5:12). The lesson was never that food did not matter. God gave them food! The lesson was that their lives depended on the God who provided it and on His word.

The tempter pressed Jesus at the point of a genuine need. That is still a familiar opening for temptation. When we are exhausted, hurting, afraid, or desperate for an answer, an offer of immediate relief can sound especially convincing. Even a claim to take dominion in Jesus’ name over your lack, pain, or desperation deserves careful attention if it urges you to pursue what you desire without regard for God’s Word. The question is not merely, “Could I have this?” but “Whose voice am I following to get it?”

Jesus did not answer the tempter by denying His hunger. Instead, He answered by trusting the Father. Bread sustains our bodies, but it cannot give us everything we need for life with God. Moses later told Israel that God’s word was “your very life” (Deuteronomy 32:47). Jesus would also tell His disciples, “The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and are life” (John 6:63). At His weakest physical moment, Jesus held fast to the word of the One who had called Him His beloved Son.

The Second Temptation: A Promise Put to the Test

The devil next took Jesus to the holy city and placed Him on a high point of the temple (Matthew 4:5). Luke names the city: Jerusalem (Luke 4:9). This was the Second Temple, the center of Jewish worship. It was a striking place to introduce another challenge to Jesus’ identity.

“If You are the Son of God, throw Yourself down,” the devil said. Then he quoted Psalm 91:11–12, a passage about God commanding His angels to protect someone from harm.

The devil’s quotation was close to the psalm, but he left out the phrase “to guard you in all your ways.” More than that, he used the promise to urge Jesus to jump. The psalm speaks of trusting God’s care; the devil turned it into a reason to manufacture danger and demand a rescue.

Jesus answered, “On the other hand, it is written, ‘You shall not put the LORD your God to the test’” (Matthew 4:7; see Deuteronomy 6:16). He did not respond by dismissing Psalm 91. He answered one appeal to Scripture with another passage that showed why the devil’s proposed action was wrong. A promise of God’s protection was no permission to jump from the temple. The psalm was never an invitation to manufacture danger.

This exchange reminds us that someone can quote Scripture and still misuse it. The problem is not always a fabricated verse. Sometimes the words are familiar, but the meaning has been pulled away from its setting. Psalm 91 is poetry that celebrates refuge in God. Its promise deserves to be taken seriously, but its images must be read in context. It does not instruct God’s people to create a crisis so God will prove Himself.

That matters when someone challenges us with a rapid stream of Bible references. It can feel intimidating if we cannot immediately recall the book, chapter, and verse. Those numbers are useful tools, but they were added long after Jesus and the New Testament writers spoke and wrote. Jesus knew the Scriptures and understood how their teaching fit together. That is a better goal than winning a contest of who can name a reference the fastest.

Colossians 3:16 urges us to let the word of Christ dwell in us richly. We do that by reading, studying, remembering, and living what it says. Then, when we need to correct a misuse of Scripture, our conduct matters too: the Lord’s servant is to be kind, able to teach, patient, and gentle rather than quarrelsome (2 Timothy 2:24–25). We can hold firmly to the truth without trying to throw each other off the temple.

I, too, struggle to memorize Scripture by book, chapter, and verse. Memorizing Scripture is a good thing, but I have learned that knowing God’s Word in the depths of my soul is something more. When temptation comes, I need more than the ability to recite a reference; I need the truth of His Word to guide my choices. Because His truth is sure, I have confidence to face today’s challenges.

The Third Temptation: An Offer at a Cost

The devil took Jesus to a very high mountain and showed Him “all the kingdoms of the world and their glory” (Matthew 4:8). This time, he did not begin with “If You are the Son of God.” His offer was direct: “All these things I will give You, if You fall down and worship me” (Matthew 4:9).

What an offer—and what a price. Luke’s account adds that the devil claimed authority to give away the kingdoms and their glory (Luke 4:6). That is the devil’s claim within the account; we need not take his word as the final account of who owns the world. What Matthew makes plain is the exchange he wanted: worship me, and the kingdoms are yours.

Jesus’ answer was equally direct: “Be gone, Satan! For it is written, ‘You shall worship the Lord your God and Him only shall you serve’” (Matthew 4:10, ESV). Jesus addressed His adversary and refused the bargain. No kingdom, however glorious, was worth giving to someone else the worship that belongs to God.

Again, Jesus answered from Deuteronomy. Deuteronomy 6:13–14 commands Israel to fear and worship the LORD and warns them against following other gods. Jesus’ words in Matthew are not a word-for-word copy of those verses, but they bring their meaning straight to the question before Him. The issue was never whether the kingdoms looked impressive. The issue was whom Jesus would worship.

Then the devil left Him, and angels came and ministered to Him (Matthew 4:11). The adversary who had offered Jesus a deal did not get His worship. Jesus had remained faithful through hunger, through a twisted appeal to God’s protection, and through an offer of worldly glory.

Faithful Through Temptation

In the wilderness, Jesus faced the temptation to satisfy His hunger apart from the Father’s will, to demand proof of God’s care, and to gain the kingdoms of the world by bowing to Satan. Each time, He answered with Scripture and remained faithful to His Father’s purpose. His example calls us to recognize tempting voices, hold fast to God’s Word, and trust Him even when another path seems easier.

When we struggle to do that, we can take heart: “We do not have a high priest who cannot sympathize with our weaknesses, but One who has been tempted in all things as we are, yet without sin” (Hebrews 4:15). Jesus took on human flesh and faced temptation. He understands our weakness, and we can come to Him for help.

Disciple’s Challenge

This week, pay attention to a moment when you feel pulled toward an easier path. Ask yourself, “Whose voice am I following?” Bring that desire before God, look to His Word, and choose one step of obedience, even if it is difficult.

Prayer

Father, thank You for sending Jesus, who understands our weakness and remained faithful through every temptation. Help us recognize voices that lead us away from Your Word. Teach us to trust You when we are weary, uncertain, or drawn toward an easier path. Let Your truth take root deep within us, so we can follow You with courage and obedience. In Jesus’ name, amen.
